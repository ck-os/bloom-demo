  • Flowering Dogwood by the porch

    Flowering Dogwood · Front Lawn

    NC native

    Sun

    Part shade — an understory tree; hot full sun stresses it and increases disease pressure

    Watering

    About 1 inch/week during establishment; established trees rarely need supplemental water.

    Pruning window

    Dormant winter season

    Wildlife value

    North Carolina's state flower; fall berries are a major food source for birds

    Regenerative practices

    • Leave fallen leaves under the canopy as natural mulch rather than raking them away
    • Avoid soil compaction and string-trimmer/mower damage at the trunk base — a common real cause of decline
    • Remove dead or diseased wood promptly to limit anthracnose spread

  • American Beautyberry, fence line

    American Beautyberry · Back Pasture

    NC native

    Sun

    Part shade to full sun

    Watering

    About 1 inch/week during establishment; drought-tolerant once established.

    Pruning window

    Late winter — cut back hard to encourage vigorous new growth

    Wildlife value

    Vivid purple berry clusters are a valuable fall/winter food source for birds

    Regenerative practices

    • Extremely low-input once established
    • Blooms and fruits on new growth — prune hard in late winter to encourage vigorous growth and better fruiting

  • Mixed pasture forage

    Tall Fescue Turf (Charlotte / Piedmont NC) · Back Pasture

    Watering

    About 1 inch of water per week, including rainfall — deep and infrequent beats daily and shallow.

    Regenerative practices

    • Grasscycle clippings back into the lawn
    • Compost topdress after fall aeration instead of a synthetic quick-release fertilizer
    • Spot-treat weeds rather than blanket herbicide or pre-emergent
    • Consider interseeding clover to fix nitrogen naturally

  • Azalea by the mailbox

    Azalea · Front Bed

    Sun

    Partial shade / dappled sun — hot afternoon sun stresses most garden hybrids

    Watering

    About 1 inch/week including rainfall — shallow roots dry out fast without mulch.

    Pruning window

    Right after bloom finishes

    Wildlife value

    An early nectar source for pollinators while in bloom

    Fertilizing (at this plant's current intensity)

    • Compost or organic acidic feed — Right after spring bloom (organic)

    Regenerative practices

    • Mulch with pine straw or pine bark to maintain the acidic soil azaleas need and retain moisture
    • Compost or organic acidifying feed instead of synthetic fertilizer
    • Watch for lace bugs, especially on plants in too much sun — a common Piedmont azalea problem

  • Gardenia

    Gardenia · Front Bed

    Sun

    Full sun to part shade — afternoon shade helps through Piedmont summer heat

    Watering

    Consistently moist, well-drained soil — don't let it dry out between waterings.

    Pruning window

    Right after flowering — avoid heavy fall pruning

    Wildlife value

    Fragrant flowers draw pollinating insects while in bloom

    Fertilizing (at this plant's current intensity)

    • Compost or organic acidic feed — After flowering finishes (organic)

    Regenerative practices

    • Mulch heavily — protects roots over winter and holds the moisture gardenias need
    • Compost or organic acidic feed instead of synthetic fertilizer
    • Watch for sooty mold following whitefly or scale — a common gardenia issue

  • Oakleaf Hydrangea

    Oakleaf Hydrangea · Front Bed

    NC native

    Sun

    Part shade — tolerates more sun than other hydrangeas with consistent moisture

    Watering

    About 1 inch/week, more during hot dry stretches — mulch reduces how often this is needed.

    Pruning window

    Right after bloom finishes — blooms on old wood

    Wildlife value

    Blooms attract pollinators; exfoliating bark and fall color add winter interest

    Regenerative practices

    • Mulch to retain moisture for its relatively shallow roots
    • Minimal fertilizer needed once established
    • Blooms on old wood — prune only right after flowering, never in late winter/spring

  • Eastern Redbud

    Eastern Redbud · Front Bed

    NC native

    Sun

    Full sun to part shade — adaptable

    Watering

    About 1 inch/week during establishment; drought-tolerant once established.

    Pruning window

    Right after bloom, only if shaping is needed — naturally attractive form

    Wildlife value

    One of the earliest nectar sources for native bees, blooming before most other plants

    Regenerative practices

    • Minimal input needed once established
    • Avoid excess fertilizer — it pushes leafy growth at the expense of next year's bloom
